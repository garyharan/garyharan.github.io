What I expect

Three things I ask of everyone

Everything else is negotiable. These three are how I keep you visible, keep you growing, and keep problems from getting expensive.

Expectation 01

Keep a weekly journal

Once a week, write a short entry: what you learned and what you accomplished. That's it. It takes ten minutes and it doesn't need to be polished.

I ask for this because I want you to be able to advocate for yourself. Nobody remembers March in November — not you, and not me. When promotion season comes around, the person with a written record of their impact walks in with evidence while everyone else walks in with vibes.

It also keeps us aligned. If I'm reading your journal, I'm never surprised by what you think your best work was, and you're never surprised by what I'm going to say about you in a room you're not in. When I make your case, I want to be making your case — not my reconstruction of it.

A good entry answers

What did I ship or move forward?
The outcome, not the activity. "Cut checkout latency by 40%" beats "worked on checkout."
What did I learn?
A technique, a part of the system, a mistake that taught you something. Learning counts even when nothing shipped.
What's in my way?
Blockers, friction, things that felt harder than they should have. This is where I can help.

Keep it wherever you like — a doc, a repo, a notebook. The habit matters more than the tool. Three bullets a week is plenty.

Expectation 02

Bring a growth mindset

I need you to believe you can get better, and then act like it. Not as a slogan — as a working method. Skill is built, not issued at birth, and the people I've seen go furthest are the ones who treated every week as a deposit.

Three things I look for:

You understand compounding

A 1% improvement that sticks beats a heroic sprint that doesn't. Small, repeated investments — in your skills, in the codebase, in the team's habits — produce returns that look impossible from the outside. The catch is that compounding only pays if you stay in the game long enough to let it work.

You reduce friction instead of tolerating it

If a process is painful, a setup step is undocumented, or the same question gets asked three times, fix it. Friction in process and friction in knowledge are the two taxes teams pay without noticing. Paying down a little of that every week is some of the highest leverage work you'll ever do — and I'll count it as real work, because it is.

You're deliberate about getting better

Know what you're currently bad at and be working on it on purpose. Ask for the code review you're dreading. Take the project slightly above your level. Feedback is data, not a verdict — and I'll give it to you straight because I think you can use it.

What I love to hear

  • "I don't know that yet — I'll go learn it."
  • "That review stung, and it was right. Here's what I changed."
  • "This step wasted an hour of everyone's week, so I automated it."
  • "I want to be better at design docs. Can I own the next one?"

What I can't stand

  • "I'm just not a frontend person."
  • "That's how we've always done it."
  • "That's not my job."
  • "It's fine, everyone knows to work around that."

Expectation 03

Tell me when things go wrong

I can't help with what I don't know about. A problem I hear about on Monday is a conversation. The same problem on Friday is a fire, and by then my options have narrowed to the expensive ones.

So bring me bad news early, and bring it plainly. You will never be punished for the telling. I don't need you to have a solution ready, and I don't need it packaged — "I think I've broken something and I'm not sure how bad it is" is a perfectly good opening line.

Why would I fire him? I just spent a fortune training him.

— the answer Henry Ford is said to have given when asked about an employee whose mistake cost the company dearly

That's genuinely how I see mistakes: as tuition already paid. But the lesson only gets purchased if the error is named out loud. A mistake that's hidden costs us the money and teaches us nothing — we just get to pay for it again later. The boldness in addressing it is the whole mechanism.

Escalate early, not neatly

Raise it while it's still small and still ugly. I'd much rather get five false alarms than miss the one that mattered.

No surprises in either direction

If a date is slipping, say so when you first suspect it — not on the deadline. I'll do the same for you: you'll hear about things that affect you from me, early, and not through the grapevine.

We fix the system, not the person

When something breaks, my first question is what let it break, not who touched it last. Blameless doesn't mean consequence-free — it means the consequence is a better system, and a lesson we all get to keep.

In short

Write it down so I can advocate for you with facts. Get better on purpose so the writing keeps getting more impressive. Tell me early so I can actually help. Do those three and I'll handle a lot of the rest.
